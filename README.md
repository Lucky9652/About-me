6. Have you ever had a conflict with a boss or professor? How was it resolved?

Sample answer (about 60-75 seconds):

“Yes, I have, though never anything major. There have been disagreements that needed to be worked out. When that happens, I first try to fully understand the other person’s perspective. I listen to their reasoning, because they often have context I don’t. Then I look for a collaborative solution that serves the shared goal.

For example, during a DevOps project in my certification program, I was building a CI/CD pipeline and wanted to use one approach for the deployment stages. My mentor suggested a different structure. At first I didn’t see why, since my version worked. So I asked him to walk me through his thinking. He explained that his approach would be easier to maintain and troubleshoot as the project grew, which was a point I hadn’t considered. I shared my reasons too, mainly that mine was faster to set up.

We agreed to keep my overall design, since I was responsible for delivering the pipeline, but I adopted his suggestions on the stage structure and error handling. When we finished, the pipeline was cleaner and more reliable than my original version, and he used it as a reference for the rest of the group. I learned that a disagreement, handled openly, can improve the result.”

The behavioral process this shows (the part the interviewer is listening for):

Stay calm and curious. Don’t get defensive.
Listen first. Ask questions to understand their reasoning.
Share your own view respectfully.
Focus on the goal, not on who is right.
Combine the best ideas and take ownership of the final result.
Close on the outcome and what you learned.

Tips:

Swap in a real example. I wrote this one as a plausible scenario based on your certification program. Replace the details with something that actually happened, whether with a mentor, professor, manager at Suyug Infra, or a project lead during your Master’s. A true story holds up better under follow-up questions.
Never badmouth anyone. Even if the other person was wrong, present their view fairly.
Pick a low-stakes conflict. A difference of approach is ideal. Avoid personal clashes or situations where you were clearly at fault.
Keep it short and relaxed. Don’t ramble. State the situation, the steps you took and the outcome.
Be ready for “What would you do if you still disagreed?” A good answer is: “I’d respect the final decision of the person responsible, commit to it fully, and raise any remaining concerns constructively.”


7. What is your greatest weakness?

Sample answer (about 45-60 seconds):

“One weakness I’ve had is documentation. When I’m deep in a project, such as building a pipeline or setting up infrastructure, I focus on getting it working and tend to leave the notes for later. A few times, when I went back to a project after a few weeks, I had to spend extra time re-learning my own setup. In a team, that would slow down everyone else too.

I’ve taken real steps to fix this. I now write a short README for every project as I build it, covering the architecture, how to run it and the problems I hit. I commit notes to GitHub together with the code, so documentation is part of my workflow. I also use Notion to log what I learn each day and a simple checklist for each task, and I don’t mark it done until the documentation is updated. If you’d like, I can show you the READMEs on my GitHub. It has made my work much easier to hand over, and it’s a habit I’ll bring to your team.”

Why this works:

It’s a true weakness, not a strength in disguise.
It’s not critical to the job. Documentation is a skill you can improve, unlike something like “I can’t work with Linux.”
It shows an action plan. The README habit, the checklist and the daily log are concrete.
It offers proof. Showing your GitHub is the DevOps version of showing a planner, so be sure the repos have READMEs before you say it.

Alternative weaknesses (if documentation doesn’t fit):

Prioritization with multiple tasks. “When several tasks came in at once, I used to try to do everything at the same time. Now I list tasks each morning, rank them by urgency and impact, and use Google Calendar and a Kanban board (Trello or Jira) to track progress. I cross items off as they’re done.”
Public speaking and presenting. “I’m more comfortable with the technical work than presenting it. I’ve been practising by explaining my projects in short walkthroughs and recording demos, and it’s getting easier each time.”

Tips:

Don’t repeat the weakness from earlier. I gave you “going too deep before asking for help” and “limited production experience” in question 3’s answer. Choose one weakness and stick with it, because interviewers sometimes ask the same thing twice in different forms.
Only claim what you really do. If you don’t yet write READMEs consistently, start now on your current projects so the answer is true and you have something to show.
End on the improvement, not the weakness.

8. What qualities do you feel a successful manager should have?

Sample answer (about 60-75 seconds):

“For me, the key quality of a successful manager is leadership, and at the heart of leadership is vision. A good manager sets the direction for the team and has a big-picture view of where the team and the company are heading. Then they break that vision into small, achievable tasks so everyone knows what to work on and how it contributes to the goal.

In a DevOps environment, that might mean a manager who says, ‘This quarter our goal is faster, more reliable deployments,’ and then helps the team get there step by step, through automation, better monitoring and fewer manual releases.

Most importantly, a successful manager is motivating. That comes from a positive, can-do attitude, especially when something breaks in production and the pressure is high. I believe the highest calling of a leader is inspiring people to reach the best of their abilities. A good leader also connects with each person individually, because everyone learns and works differently. They give credit to the team, take responsibility when things go wrong, and create a space where people feel safe asking questions.

Personal example: I’ve seen this in my own life. During my cloud and DevOps program, my mentor didn’t just teach us the tools. He showed us where the industry was heading and why each skill mattered. When I was struggling with Kubernetes, he didn’t hand me the answer. He asked guiding questions and gave me small tasks that built up my understanding, and he encouraged me when I was moving from a mechanical background into cloud. His belief in me gave me the confidence to keep going and earn my certification. I try to carry that into my own work: give people a clear direction, trust them, and help them grow. Those are my thoughts on it.”

Quality checklist (what the interviewer wants to hear):

Leadership and vision: clear direction, a big-picture view and small achievable tasks
Motivation: a positive, can-do attitude that inspires others
Individual connection: treating each person according to how they work best
Accountability: crediting the team and owning the problems

Tips:

Replace the example with a real person. I wrote the mentor story as a plausible scenario based on your certification program. Use someone who actually influenced you, such as a professor from your Master’s at Anna University, a lead at Suyug Infra, a family member, or a teacher. A true story sounds natural and holds up under follow-up questions.
Be specific about what they did, not just that they were “great.” Say what behavior changed you and how it shaped you.
Keep it positive. Don’t compare the person to a bad manager you’ve had.
Pause before the example. It makes the shift from the general answer to your story feel natural.

Structure (so you can adapt it on the spot):

Differentiator: passion for excellence plus an engineer’s reliability mindset.
Proof: a specific project example.
Close: commitment and growth.

Tips:

Be ready to back up the example. Only describe project details that are true for you (the Terraform, Docker and Grafana setup, the GitHub documentation), and be ready for follow-ups such as “What problem did you hit?” or “Show me the repo.”

Say it with conviction. The confidence is part of the answer, so keep your tone steady and don’t sound arrogant.
Don’t run it together with your earlier answer. If you get both “Why should we hire you?” and “What sets you apart?”, use this as the main answer and keep the skills-and-learning version as a backup.
